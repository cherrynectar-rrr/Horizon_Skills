# Habit Change

Version: 0.1  
Status: Candidate — Trial  
Owner: Horizon Core  
Created: 2026-09-05

## Purpose

Turn a recurring everyday-life friction into a small, testable behavior-change experiment.

The Skill should reduce dependence on willpower and avoid building a second productivity bureaucracy around habits.

## Trigger

Use when the user asks to:

- build a routine;
- stop or reduce an ordinary unwanted habit;
- become more consistent;
- fix sleep-timing, phone-use, study-start or exercise-start friction;
- replace an automatic behavior with a better one;
- recover after repeatedly failing a simple routine.

## Safety Boundary

This is everyday behavior coaching, not clinical treatment.

Do not treat with a habit plan alone when the behavior involves:

- self-harm;
- substance addiction;
- eating disorders;
- clinically significant compulsions or anxiety-driven rituals;
- dangerous sleepiness or another health issue needing medical evaluation;
- behavior causing substantial injury or functional impairment.

In those cases, ordinary habit mechanics may be supportive but should not replace appropriate professional care.

## Core Principle

> **Change the behavior system before blaming motivation.**

A useful plan changes one or more of:

- capability — can the person realistically do the behavior?
- opportunity — does the environment make it easy or hard?
- motivation — is there a clear reason / immediate consequence?

This is compatible with the COM-B framing used in behaviour-change guidance.

## Minimum Intake

Collect only information that changes the intervention:

1. **Target behavior** — specific and observable.
2. **Direction** — build, reduce, stop or replace.
3. **Trigger / context** — when, where and around what event it happens.
4. **Current payoff** — what the existing behavior immediately provides or avoids.
5. **Friction** — what makes the desired behavior hard.
6. **Past attempts** — what repeatedly caused failure.
7. **Capacity** — realistic time / energy in the current week.

## Default Workflow

```text
observe the real behavior
→ define one small target
→ identify trigger + friction
→ redesign environment
→ write an explicit action plan
→ add a recovery / if-then plan
→ use minimal monitoring
→ review from evidence
→ scale, modify or stop
```

### 1. Define one observable behavior

Replace vague targets:

- "be disciplined"
- "stop wasting time"
- "sleep better"

with actions that can actually be observed.

Examples of shape:

- phone remains outside the desk area during one study block;
- after brushing teeth, prepare the next morning's water bottle;
- when the urge to bite nails appears during study, use a chosen substitute action;
- begin the target routine with a two-minute entry action.

Examples are not universal prescriptions.

### 2. Start with the smallest change that can produce information

Do not stack five new routines at once.

Shrink the first action until it is realistic under the user's actual schedule. Small, manageable routine changes are easier to test and maintain than an ambitious life overhaul.

The first version is an experiment, not a permanent identity statement.

### 3. Map trigger and opportunity

Ask what reliably happens immediately before the behavior.

Possible triggers:

- location;
- time;
- another routine;
- boredom / stress;
- opening a device;
- beginning difficult study;
- lying in bed;
- seeing an object.

Then change the environment where possible:

- make the desired action visible / convenient;
- add friction to the unwanted action;
- prepare tools beforehand;
- remove unnecessary decisions;
- use an existing routine as an anchor only when the anchor is genuinely stable.

Environment design is normally preferable to repeated motivational reminders.

### 4. Write an explicit action plan

Specify:

- what behavior;
- in what situation;
- where;
- approximately when / after what cue.

Use an implementation-style statement when helpful:

`When / after X, I will do Y at Z.`

### 5. Add a coping / recovery plan

Predict the most likely failure mode before it happens.

Use an explicit if-then recovery rule:

`If <known obstacle> happens, then I will <small fallback / restart action>.`

Avoid streak perfectionism. A missed day is evidence about the system; the goal is fast recovery, not protecting a number on a tracker.

### 6. Monitor minimally

Track only enough to answer:

- Did the target behavior happen?
- In what context did it fail?
- Did the environment change help?

A checkbox, quick count or short note may be enough.

Do not require a dashboard, daily journal or complicated score unless a real question needs it.

### 7. Review and adapt

At a short review interval, classify the failure:

- target too large;
- trigger unreliable;
- environment still favors the old behavior;
- substitute behavior does not satisfy the same immediate need;
- time / energy assumption unrealistic;
- behavior is not actually worth maintaining.

Change the intervention before expanding it.

Scale only when the small version is reliably working and additional scale is useful.

## Useful Technique Set

Prefer techniques with a clear purpose:

- specific behavioral goals;
- action planning;
- if-then coping / relapse plans;
- environmental restructuring;
- self-monitoring / feedback when useful;
- social support when it actually helps;
- small manageable routine changes;
- replacement behaviors for unwanted routines.

Do not use identity slogans, rewards or streaks as mandatory doctrine. They may help some people but are optional tools, not the mechanism of every habit.

## Provenance

Method sources inspected:

- `JayRHa/AgentSkills` — `habit-builder` (MIT);
- NICE PH49, `Behaviour change: individual approaches`, for goals/planning, feedback/monitoring, coping plans, environmental changes and maintenance principles.

Absorbed from the community Skill:

- behavior specificity;
- cue / context mapping;
- environment design;
- tiny starting actions;
- replacement behavior;
- regular review.

Strengthened / corrected for Horizon:

- replace rigid "never miss twice" / streak logic with explicit recovery plans;
- avoid treating identity framing or immediate rewards as universal requirements;
- use capability / opportunity / motivation as a diagnostic frame;
- minimize tracking overhead;
- preserve a strict boundary around clinically significant behavior.

## Success Test

The Skill succeeds when a real everyday behavior becomes easier to start, easier to resume after disruption and less dependent on repeated motivation or AI prompts.

If the habit system itself becomes a large project, simplify it.
